# Previous Research: 

My research on the police departments use of facial recognition is divided into four separate sections. The first section asks how accurately is the technology able to identify different faces. Buolamwini and Gebru (2018) found that the commercial systems often got dark skinned women wrong compared to lighter skinned men, and Grother, Ngan and Hanoka (2019) confirmed in a federal study that most algorithms mostly produce false matches for some of the demographic groups compared to the others.  Turner Lee and Chin Rothmann(2022) argues that these errors fall the hardest on the communities of color, whom the police already keeps an eye on closely. These studies really matter to my project because they explain why a match alone isn't enough to prove who someone is
The second group talks about what happens when the police treats a match as a proof. Hill (2020) reports show how the Detroit police arrested Robert Williams after a software matched his old license photo to a shoplifting suspect, while Hu (2025) showed that during the man hunt after the killing  of UnitedHealthcare CEO Brian Thompson, facial recognition did help a little and a human tip led the police to Luigi Mangione.
Together all these cases let me compare how a police department use the scans and how the court later evaluates them. The third group th then maps out how the rules change depending on the place. Garvie, Bedoya, and Frankle (2016) sent record requests to more than 100 law enforcement agencies and found that the police using face recognition was almost completely unregulated. Richardson (2021) describes the policies that the cities and states adapted in response and Laperruqe (2025) reports  that by the end of 2024, 15 states limited police use, some by requiring a warrant and others by allowing searches to happen only for serious crimes.  This group supplies the comparison of the states and the cities at the the center of my argument. The final section shows how American practices compare to those around the world. Feldstein (2029) argues that the security and surveillance should only be allowed when an independent organization approves it and checks for misuse. Rhan et al. (2025) documents how authorities in Xinjiang uses facial recognition to monitor ethnic minorities. These last sources go on to show the consequences and misuse of power that happens when no independent checks are done on and that they help me explain the rules that American police departments and courts should follow.
