## Research Notes

### Note 1: Speed and Efficiency at the Airport

**Quotation:** TSA spokesperson R. Carter Langston explained that passengers appreciate the system because of its “speed” and “efficiency” (Compton 2026).

**Commentary:** This article shows how biometric identification can make the travel process way faster and more convenient for people. Instead of spending time searching for identification documents, travelers are able to move through security lines way more quickly while still having their identity verified to ensure safety. As a result, AI surveillance systems can improve efficiency in places that require large amounts of security screening.

**Subject terms:** biometric screening; efficiency; TSA PreCheck

### Note 2: Prohibited in Principle

**Quotation:** “The use of biometric identification systems (RBI) by law enforcement is prohibited in principle, except in exhaustively listed and narrowly defined situations.” (European Parliament 2024)

**Commentary:** This shows that lawmakers recognize that biometric identification systems can help improve security when they are used responsibly and under strict regulations, but that AI surveillance can be dangerous if left unchecked. Instead of putting a complete ban on the technology, the European Union allows its use in situations where it can help protect people while still respecting citizens’ rights. By restricting when and how these systems can be used, the EU is trying to prevent abuse while still allowing the technology to be used when necessary.

**Subject terms:** EU AI Act; biometric identification; narrow exception; regulation

### Note 3: When an Error Costs Only a Second Try

**Quotation:** “In cooperative access control applications, false negatives can be remedied by users making second attempts.” (Grother, Ngan, and Hanaoka 2019, 3)

**Commentary:** NIST explains why facial recognition works well at a checkpoint like the one in Note 1. A traveler who is not recognized can simply try again, so an error costs a few seconds. A police search is different: the person in the photo is not there to try again, and a false match can cost them their freedom.

**Subject terms:** biometric screening; false negative; access control

### Note 4: The Computer Got It Wrong

**Quotation:** “I guess the computer got it wrong” (Hill 2020)

**Commentary:** This evidence shows that facial recognition technology is not always reliable and that the mistakes made by AI systems can have serious consequences for innocent people. In Williams’s case, he was arrested, fingerprinted, and jailed even though he had done nothing wrong. This shows how relying too heavily on AI technology can lead to wrongful accusations and violations of people’s rights.

**Subject terms:** wrongful arrest; misidentification; human verification

### Note 5: Surveillance Aimed at a Minority

**Quotation:** “Xinjiang authorities have blanketed the region in facial-recognition cameras, forced residents to install surveillance apps on their phones and implemented a ‘predictive policing’ regime that uses mass data collection and analysis to flag behaviour deemed suspicious” (Ryan et al. 2025, 50).

**Commentary:** This evidence shows how AI surveillance can be used to unfairly monitor and target specific groups of people. Although these systems can benefit people, they can also contribute to discrimination, loss of privacy, and government overreach. The residents are constantly being monitored through cameras, smartphones, and large databases created to collect personal information. This example demonstrates how AI surveillance can be used for purposes that go beyond public safety and could become a tool for monitoring an entire population.

**Subject terms:** ethnic minorities; mass surveillance; predictive policing; privacy

### Note 6: Bias Across Most Algorithms

**Quotation:** “We found empirical evidence for the existence of demographic differentials in the majority of contemporary face recognition algorithms that we evaluated.” (Grother, Ngan, and Hanaoka 2019, 6)

**Commentary:** If biased data is being used to train an AI system, then the technology will continue to produce unfair results. This can lead to certain groups being monitored more frequently or incorrectly identified at higher rates than others. As a result, existing bias may become even worse when AI systems are used without proper testing. NIST’s finding shows this problem is widespread, not limited to one product.

**Subject terms:** demographic differential; algorithmic bias; testing

### Note 7: Independent Oversight

**Quotation:** Feldstein explains that legitimate surveillance requires “a robust, independent oversight system” that can authorize surveillance measures and provide remedies when abuses occur (Feldstein 2019, 12).

**Commentary:** This shows that surveillance systems should not be allowed to operate without supervision and full accountability. Instead, governments need to create systems of oversight that ensure AI technologies are being used fairly and legally. Without these protections, AI surveillance can easily violate privacy and people’s rights and also lead to wrongful identification.

**Subject terms:** independent oversight; accountability; legitimate surveillance

### Note 8: The Hybrid Machine-Human System

**Quotation:** “Algorithms used in this way are part of a hybrid machine-human system: The algorithm offers up candidates for human adjudication, for which labor must be available.” (Grother, Ngan, and Hanaoka 2019, 5)

**Commentary:** Human verification is especially important because every situation is different. A facial recognition system may be able to compare images, but it can’t fully grasp the context and analyze in depth like a human can. Human reviewers can examine additional evidence, consider various different circumstances, and determine whether the technology’s conclusion makes sense.

**Subject terms:** human verification; candidate list; human review

### Note 9: Testing Every Group

**Quotation:** “As most systems are configured with a fixed threshold, it is necessary to report both false negative and false positive rates for each demographic group at that threshold.” (Grother, Ngan, and Hanaoka 2019, 3)

**Commentary:** Regular testing will allow experts to identify bias, discrimination, and false identification before they cause harm. If governments and companies are required to regularly evaluate their systems, that can improve accuracy while protecting the rights of citizens. NIST’s point shows what that testing must measure: error rates for each group, not just one overall score.

**Subject terms:** testing; demographic differential; accountability

### Note 10: From False Positive to False Accusation 

**Quotation:** “In identification applications such as visa or passport fraud detection, or surveillance, a false positive match to another individual could lead to a false accusation, detention or deportation.” (Grother, Ngan, and Hanaoka 2019, 5)

**Commentary:** This is a serious issue because technology used by police needs to be accurate, as it can lead to an innocent person being charged and harmed. NIST names surveillance directly, and the consequences it lists, false accusation and detention, are exactly what happened to Robert Williams in Note 4.

**Subject terms:** false positive; misidentification; wrongful arrest
