# Claim and Storyboard: 

## Claim paragraph

Police departments across the US now use facial recognition to search for suspects by comparing a photo from the security camera against databases of mugshots and driver’s licenses photos. Most of the departments say that they treat a match only as an a way to have a lead for the investigation rather than as proof or identity, and many people assume that the courts will catch any error that slips through. Yet the rules that govern these searches change from one state and city to the next. Some of the states require a warrant or allow the searches to only happen for serious crimes, while many other states have no law at all and only a few require the prosecutor to tell the defendants that facial recognition had a role in their case (Laperruque 2025). As a result, a persons protection against a false match ends up depending on where the person happens to live. For example. Detroit police arrested Robert Williams for a theft that he did not commit after a computer matched his photo (Hill 2020), and even the 2024 manhunt for Luigi Mangione, which ended due to a human tip rather than a compute match showed just how little the public knows about when and how police run these searches (Hu 2025). I argue that because a facial recognition match is unreliable  and is treated different depending on the place, every state should take in the same minimum safeguards such as no arrest based on a match alone, independent evidence to confirm every march and full disclosure of facial recognition use to the defendants and the courts.

## Storyboard

**Claim:** Because a facial recognition match is unreliable as proof and is treated inconsistently from place to place, every state should adopt the same minimum safeguards: no arrest on a match alone, independent corroboration, and full disclosure to defendants and courts.

**Warrant (the general principle connecting the reasons to the claim):** People facing the same technology and the same risk of error deserve the same legal protection, no matter where they live.

### Section 1: Background (how police facial recognition works)
- **Operative point:** A police search does not "identify" anyone. It returns a ranked list of possible candidates that a human analyst must review, which is why departments call the result a "lead."
- **Evidence:** Garvie, Bedoya, and Frankle (2016) on how police run searches against mugshot and license-photo databases and how many adults are enrolled; the NYPD's description of a match as an investigative lead, not a conclusive identification.
- **Key terms:** probe image, one-to-many search, candidate list, investigative lead, misidentification.

### Section 2: Reason 1. A match is not reliable enough to serve as proof.
- **Operative reason:** Error rates rise with poor image quality and vary by race, sex, and age, so a match can point confidently at the wrong person.
- **Evidence:** Buolamwini and Gebru (2018) on error rates of up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women compared with 0.8% for lighter-skinned men; Grother, Ngan, and Hanaoka (2019) on demographic differences in most algorithms; masked and partial images in the Mangione search (Hu 2025); Turner Lee and Chin-Rothmann (2022) on who bears the cost.
- **Key terms:** false positive, demographic differential, image quality, algorithmic bias.

### Section 3: Reason 2. Inside police departments, a "lead" often becomes the basis for an arrest.
- **Operative reason:** Department policies call a match a lead, but in practice officers may skip the follow-up investigation, and few departments audit their own use.
- **Evidence:** The Robert Williams arrest (Hill 2020); the ACLU count of more than a dozen wrongful arrests linked to facial recognition; Garvie, Bedoya, and Frankle (2016) on how few agencies had public policies or audits; the contrast in the Mangione case, where human recognition (a McDonald's customer and an Altoona officer), not software, produced the identification (Hu 2025).
- **Key terms:** departmental policy, corroboration, human review, automation bias, audit.

### Section 4: Reason 3. Protection depends on location (state and city differences).
- **Operative reason:** With no federal law, states and cities set their own rules, so the same search can be forbidden in one place and routine in another.
- **Evidence:** Laperruque (2025) on 15 states with limits by the end of 2024: warrants in Montana and Utah, serious-crime limits in Maine, Maryland, Utah, and Vermont; Maryland's statute barring identification based solely on a match; Richardson (2021) on city and state policies; city bans (for example, San Francisco and Boston, still to verify).
- **Key terms:** patchwork, warrant requirement, serious-crime limit, local ban, jurisdiction.

### Section 5: Reason 4. Courts treat a scan differently from police, and defendants often never learn one was used.
- **Operative reason:** Prosecutors rarely offer a match as evidence at trial, so its role stays hidden unless the law requires disclosure. Only some states require it.
- **Evidence:** *State v. Arteaga* (N.J. App. Div. 2023) and the June 2026 New Jersey Supreme Court ruling requiring disclosure (EPIC 2026); disclosure laws in Colorado, Maryland, and Washington (CDT); Maryland's rule that evidence obtained in violation of the statute is inadmissible; the Mangione defense's challenge to the identification at the December 2025 suppression hearing.
- **Key terms:** admissibility, discovery and disclosure, *Brady* obligation, suppression, exclusionary rule.

### Section 6: Acknowledgment and response (the strongest objection)
- **Objection:** Facial recognition saves time and solves crimes, for example TSA Touchless ID, missing-person cases, and narrow uses allowed under the EU AI Act. Strict rules could slow investigations.
- **Response:** The safeguards proposed here do not ban the technology. They keep it as a lead and require humans to confirm it. The EU allows narrow use under strict conditions, and the Mangione arrest shows that ordinary police work still succeeds.
- **Evidence:** Compton (2026); European Parliament (2024); Hu (2025).
- **Key terms:** proportionality, narrow exception, public safety.

### Section 7: Reason 5. Uniform safeguards are the solution.
- **Operative reason:** Minimum national standards remove the location problem: no arrest on a match alone, independent corroboration, disclosure to defendants, regular accuracy testing, and officer training.
- **Evidence:** Feldstein (2019) on independent oversight as the test of legitimate surveillance; Maryland and Montana/Utah as working state models; Ryan et al. (2025) on Xinjiang as a warning about surveillance with no independent check.
- **Key terms:** independent oversight, human verification, transparency, uniform standard.

### Conclusion
A person's protection from a false match should not depend on their zip code. Point to the Second Note Collection for further research (for example, newer wrongful-arrest cases and the outcome of the Mangione trial).
