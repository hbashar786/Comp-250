# Part 1: Topic, Question, and Problem

I am studying how police departments in the United States use facial recognition technology to identify criminal suspects, because I want to find out what verification steps, if any, departments require before officers treat a facial recognition match as grounds for an arrest, in order to help my readers understand whether current safeguards are strong enough to keep innocent people from being arrested because of an algorithm's mistake.
This question matters because facial recognition systems make mistakes, and they make them unevenly.
When the National Institute of Standards and Technology tested face recognition algorithms in 2019, it found that many of them falsely matched Black and Asian faces at higher rates than white faces (Grother et al., 2019).
When officers act on a false match without checking it against other evidence, an innocent person pays the cost.
In 2020, Detroit police arrested Robert Williams in front of his family and held him overnight after software wrongly matched his driver's license photo to a shoplifting suspect (Hill, 2020).
If departments continue to treat a match as proof rather than as a lead, more people like Williams will lose their freedom, their reputations, and their trust in the police.
Lawmakers and police leaders therefore need to know which verification policies actually prevent these errors, so that they can require those policies wherever police use facial recognition.
